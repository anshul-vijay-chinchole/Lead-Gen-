# Legal and terms-of-service constraints on reselling job-posting and B2B contact data (Hiring Signal Report)

**Not legal advice.** This is a practical risk map compiled from provider terms pages, court and regulator material, and law-firm commentary, current to September 2026. A lawyer in each target market should check it before the business sells data.

**Method caveat for the report writer.** The research sandbox blocked direct page fetches for almost every domain (adzuna.com, apollo.io, hunter.io, ico.org.uk, cppa.ca.gov, ftc.gov, law-firm sites). So the findings below come from search-engine extracts of the cited pages, not full reads. Contract wording that the business will rely on, especially provider resale clauses, should be re-read on the live page before relying on it. Where a claim comes from background knowledge and could not be confirmed in this session, it is marked "(not verified this session)" or placed under Gaps.

**Repository context (checked in the repo).** The engine's default delivery path uses an Adzuna free key for jobs, offline email-pattern guessing for work emails (`leadgen/enrich/pattern.py`) and an offline email check. Optional connectors exist for TheirStack, Apollo, Hunter, Greenhouse/Lever/Ashby, Apify and `linkedin_jobs`. The last is flagged "use at own risk" and is not in any default playbook (`/home/user/Lead-Gen-/CONTEXT.md`, `/home/user/Lead-Gen-/PLAN.md` section 6). The engine has do-not-lists (`leadgen suppress`) but no delete-a-person command and no automatic retention purge (`CONTEXT.md`, "Gaps to know").

## 1. Data-provider terms on redistribution and resale

### Takeaway
The two most likely contact-data sources, Apollo and Hunter, forbid reselling or passing their data to third parties under standard terms. Adzuna's API terms allow commercial use only for a 14-day evaluation trial and require written consent or a licence for ongoing commercial use. That means the repo's default "free Adzuna key" path is not a licence to sell weekly reports. Public ATS job-board APIs (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby) are unauthenticated and built for publishing jobs. No explicit resale licence was found for them, but they carry little personal data. Resale rights must be bought through a negotiated data licence (Coresignal says its data contracts are separate agreements; TheirStack and PredictLeads terms could not be retrieved). Indeed and LinkedIn prohibit scraping outright.

### Cited Findings

**Adzuna (API, the repo's default job source)**
- API users must label each displayed advert "Jobs by Adzuna" at a minimum size of 116 x 23 pixels, with the word "Jobs" hyperlinked to adzuna.co.uk or the relevant local domain. — [Adzuna API Terms of Service](https://developer.adzuna.com/docs/terms_of_service)
- Commercial, government or academic organisations (including affiliates and individuals) may use the API only under a 14-day trial, strictly to validate data coverage, quality and usability. After the trial a licence agreement may be required. — [Adzuna API Terms of Service](https://developer.adzuna.com/docs/terms_of_service)
- The data may not be used "in its original format or in aggregation (including vacancy counts and average salaries) to deliver ongoing work or research without written consent". Usage that appears to extract confidential information for commercial reuse is treated as a breach. — [Adzuna API Terms of Service](https://developer.adzuna.com/docs/terms_of_service)

**Apollo.io (contacts and jobs connector)**
- Apollo grants a non-exclusive, non-transferable, non-sublicensable licence to use the Services "solely for internal business purposes". Users may not resell, distribute or create derivative works from the Service or the "Output Data", and may not resell or re-license the Output Data "in any manner or form". — [Apollo Terms of Service](https://www.apollo.io/terms) (search extract; the full page could not be fetched)
- API access is likewise licensed "solely for internal business purposes", and users may not sublicense, sell or distribute the APIs. — [Apollo API Terms](https://www.apollo.io/terms/api)
- Apollo publishes a GDPR Data Processing Addendum and a processing notice for the people in its database, which shows it treats itself as subject to GDPR transparency duties. — [Apollo DPA](https://www.apollo.io/dpa); [Apollo processing notice](https://www.apollo.io/privacy-policy/processing-notice)

**Hunter.io (email finder connector)**
- Hunter's terms prohibit customers from "reselling or otherwise distributing or disclosing" Hunter data, whether delivered directly or as a derived product or service, to any third party. Redistribution of any part of the service is not allowed unless an agreement with Hunter expressly permits it. — [Hunter Terms of Service](https://www.hunter.io/terms-of-service) (search extract)
- Hunter publishes a DPA and a California-specific privacy notice. — [Hunter DPA](https://hunter.io/data-processing-agreement); [Hunter California notice](https://hunter.io/privacy-policy/california)

**TheirStack (optional job and technographic source)**
- TheirStack sells job-posting and technographic data through an API with plans from a free tier upward. — [TheirStack pricing](https://theirstack.com/en/pricing)
- TheirStack publishes an "integration guide for sales intelligence software", which suggests it supports its data being embedded in other vendors' products. The resale terms themselves were not retrieved. — [TheirStack integration guide](https://theirstack.com/en/docs/guides/sales-software-integration-guide)
- Caution: one search result presented terms from **StackScan**, a different company, which forbid resale into "lead databases". Those terms are not TheirStack's and should not be attributed to it. — [StackScan terms](https://www.stackscan.com/terms)

**Greenhouse / Lever / Ashby public job-board APIs (optional sources)**
- Greenhouse's Job Board API is "designed to export information about your public job boards and job posts so your web developers can build custom career and application sites". GET endpoints need no authentication because the data is public. — [Greenhouse Job Board API docs](https://docs.greenhouse.io/job-board.html); [Greenhouse API overview](https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/10568627186203-Greenhouse-API-overview)
- Lever's Postings API is documented for building job-listing sites and needs no authentication. It exposes only active public postings, not candidate data. — [Lever postings-api (GitHub)](https://github.com/lever/postings-api); [Lever developer docs](https://hire.lever.co/developer/documentation); [Knit on Lever API](https://www.getknit.dev/blog/lever-api-directory)
- Ashby's Job Postings API lets an organisation get all its published postings to populate its own careers page. — [Ashby Job Postings API](https://developers.ashbyhq.com/docs/public-job-posting-api)
- Third-party aggregators that re-serve these feeds tell their users to respect "the source's terms, and job-posting rights in your use and redistribution", and to comply with GDPR and CCPA. — [JobsPipe on Greenhouse](https://jobspipe.dev/sources/greenhouse)

**Coresignal and PredictLeads (licensable datasets)**
- Coresignal's website Terms and Conditions state that they do not govern customers who purchase data. That relationship is set by a separate signed data agreement. — [Coresignal Terms and Conditions](https://coresignal.com/terms-and-conditions/)
- Coresignal's data powers "hundreds of customers'" own data products and enrichment platforms. Resale and white-label rights are not published and are negotiated case by case. — [Explorium review of Coresignal](https://www.explorium.ai/compare/coresignal/)
- PredictLeads sells a Job Openings dataset (history since 2018, more than 220M records, about 8.5M active jobs, sourced from company career pages and ATS integrations) through API, flat files and webhooks. No public licence or redistribution terms were found. — [PredictLeads job openings](https://predictleads.com/job_listings); [PredictLeads data model](https://docs.predictleads.com/v3/data_models/job_openings_dataset)

**Indeed and LinkedIn (scraping)**
- Indeed's terms prohibit using "robots, spiders, or other automated means to access the Services for any purpose" without permission. — [Indeed Terms of Service](https://www.indeed.com/legal) (search extract)
- LinkedIn User Agreement s.8.2 ("Don'ts"), effective 3 November 2025, prohibits developing, supporting or using "software, devices, scripts, robots or any other means or processes (including crawlers, browser plugins and add-ons or any other technology) to scrape the Services or otherwise copy profiles and other data". — [LinkedIn User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement)

### Inferences
- **Apollo and Hunter cannot be the source of resold contact data under self-serve terms.** Putting Apollo "Output Data" or Hunter results into a paid client file is the redistribution both terms forbid. Using them only internally, for example to confirm a company domain that then feeds the founder's own research, is a grey area. It likely still counts as a "derived product" under Hunter's wording. A reseller or OEM agreement would be needed.
- **Adzuna is the biggest immediate contract risk for the repo's default path.** A paid weekly report built on Adzuna job data is "ongoing work" and commercial reuse. That needs a written Adzuna licence, and the "Jobs by Adzuna" attribution must appear on any displayed advert. Build attribution into the CSV/Excel/HTML outputs and contact Adzuna for a commercial or data licence before the first paid delivery.
- **Public ATS feeds are the lowest-contract-risk job source,** but they are not risk-free. The APIs exist so employers can publish jobs, and no licence expressly grants third-party resale. Job-ad text may be the employer's copyright. Lower-risk practice: deliver facts and signals (company, role title, location, date first seen, link back to the posting) rather than copying full ad text.
- **For contact data at scale, buy a licence that expressly allows redistribution to your clients** (for example a Coresignal-type data agreement or a reseller tier). Otherwise do your own research from sources whose terms allow it, such as company "team" pages, press releases and Companies House officer data, while still meeting privacy law (section 3).
- **Email verifiers (ZeroBounce, MillionVerifier, NeverBounce)** act as processors of addresses you upload rather than as data sources. Their terms matter mainly for acceptable use and data-processing terms, not resale. They were not researched in depth.

### Gaps
- Full, current wording of Adzuna, Apollo, Hunter, Indeed and TheirStack terms could not be fetched. Exact clause numbers and any 2026 revisions are unverified.
- Whether Adzuna offers a paid commercial licence tier, and at what price, is unknown. Contact Adzuna directly.
- No official Greenhouse, Lever or Ashby "API terms of use" governing third-party consumption of the public job-board endpoints was found. Their customer terms may say something about it. This remains an open question.
- PredictLeads' and TheirStack's licence terms on redistribution were not found. Ask their sales teams whether client-facing redistribution is allowed and on which plan.

## 2. Scraping case law (US and UK)

### Takeaway
In the US, scraping **public, logged-off** pages is probably not a federal crime under the CFAA after *Van Buren* (2021) and *hiQ v. LinkedIn* (9th Cir. 2022). Two things are still actionable: breaching a site's terms you agreed to (hiQ lost on contract and paid a $500k consent judgment in December 2022), and using fake accounts or logged-in access (Proxycurl was shut down in 2025). *Meta v. Bright Data* (January 2024) held that Meta's terms did not bar logged-off scraping by a non-user, but that is a single district-court ruling. In the UK, scraping also meets sui generis database right and an untested Computer Misuse Act. On both sides of the Atlantic the more likely enforcement risk for a people-data reseller is privacy law, not the scraping itself: CNIL fined Kaspr for LinkedIn contact scraping, and the UK Upper Tribunal found UK GDPR can reach a foreign scraper (Clearview, 2025).

### Cited Findings

**CFAA after *Van Buren* (settled at Supreme Court level, with an open question)**
- *Van Buren v. United States* (2021, 6-3): CFAA liability "stems from a gates-up-or-down inquiry". Either you are authorised to enter a part of a system or you are not; your purpose does not matter. — [Proskauer New Media & Technology Law blog](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2021/06/06/supreme-court-ends-long-running-circuit-split-over-cfaa-exceeds-authorized-access-issue-adopting-a-narrow-interpretation-that-will-reverberate-in-scraping-disputes-and-litigation-ov/); [ACS Law analysis](https://www.acslaw.org/analysis/acs-journal/2020-2021-acs-supreme-court-review/the-computer-fraud-and-abuse-act-after-van-buren/)
- The Court expressly left open whether the "gate" can be set by contract (terms of service) or only by technical barriers such as passwords. — [Apify summary of Van Buren](https://blog.apify.com/van-buren-v-united-states/); [ACS Law](https://www.acslaw.org/analysis/acs-journal/2020-2021-acs-supreme-court-review/the-computer-fraud-and-abuse-act-after-van-buren/)
- EFF argued to the Ninth Circuit that *Van Buren* does not criminalise scraping of public web data. — [EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/07/eff-ninth-circuit-recent-supreme-court-decision-van-buren-does-not-criminalize-web)

***hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn* (final outcome)**
- April 2022: on remand after *Van Buren*, the Ninth Circuit again held that scraping publicly available website data likely falls outside the CFAA's "without authorization". Authorisation matters for password-protected sites or sites that otherwise keep the public out. — [Proskauer, April 2022](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/04/21/taking-cue-from-the-supreme-courts-van-buren-decision-ninth-circuit-releases-new-opinion-holding-scraping-of-publicly-available-website-data-falls-outside-of-cfaa/); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/web-scraping-website-terms-and-cfaa-hiqs-preliminary-injunction-affirmed-again)
- November 2022: the district court granted LinkedIn summary judgment on breach of contract. hiQ's scraping, and its use of fake profiles ("turkers"), breached LinkedIn's User Agreement. — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/linkedin-s-data-scraping-battle-hiq-labs-ends-proposed-judgment); [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/12/as-everyone-expected-years-ago-hiqs-cfaa-wins-dont-mean-it-can-freely-scrape-hiq-v-linkedin-guest-blog-post-part-1-of-2.htm)
- 7-8 December 2022: in a stipulated consent judgment, hiQ agreed to a $500,000 judgment for breach of contract, a CFAA violation and other claims. A permanent injunction required hiQ to stop scraping LinkedIn and destroy its source code, data and algorithms derived from scraped data. — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/linkedin-s-data-scraping-battle-hiq-labs-ends-proposed-judgment); [Proskauer, Dec 2022](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/12/08/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case/); [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/alternative-data/hiq-v-linkedin-wrapped-up-web-scraping-lessons-learned/); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2022/12/linkedin-v-hiq-landmark-data-scraping-suit-provides-guidance-to-data-scrapers-and-web-operators)

***Meta Platforms v. Bright Data* (N.D. Cal., January 2024)**
- On 23-24 January 2024 Judge Chen granted Bright Data summary judgment. The Facebook and Instagram terms "do not apply to and prohibit Bright Data's scraping of publicly available data while logged off". A party that is not logged in is not a "user" bound by the terms. — [Quinn Emanuel](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/news-events/client-alert-meta-v-bright-data-significant-decision-for-web-scraping-industry/); [Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/federal-judge-rules-against-meta-in-data-scraping-case/); [Farella Braun + Martel](https://www.fbm.com/publications/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-and-online-data-collection-meta-platforms-v-bright-data/)
- The court gave weight to Meta's 2009 removal of language that bound all visitors, users or not, to its terms. — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/392920/judge-sides-against-meta-in-battle-over-scraping-p.html?edition=); [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/01/game-on-bright-data-scores-major-victory-in-web-scraping-dispute-with-meta-guest-blog-post.htm)

**LinkedIn v. Proxycurl (2025)**
- LinkedIn/Microsoft sued Proxycurl on 24 January 2025 (N.D. Cal.), alleging it ran "hundreds of thousands" of fake accounts to collect member data and resold it through an API. Proxycurl settled and shut down its API on 4 July 2025. A permanent injunction required deletion of the LinkedIn data and an end to unauthorised access. — [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-wins-legal-case-data-scrapers-proxycurl/756101/); [StartupHub.ai](https://www.startuphub.ai/ai-news/startup-news/2025/the-1-linkedin-scraping-startup-proxycurl-shuts-down)

**Privacy-regulator action against scrapers (EU and UK)**
- CNIL (France), 5 December 2024: KASPR was fined €240,000. It sold a Chrome extension that pulled professional contact details from LinkedIn profiles into a database of about 160M contacts. Breaches found: collecting details of users who had limited their contact-info visibility, keeping data for 5 years from each update, no information to data subjects until 2022, then English-only notices. KASPR was ordered to stop and delete. — [CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/en/data-scraping-kaspr-fined-eu240000); [EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/data-scraping-french-supervisory-authority-fined-kaspr-eu240-000_en)
- Conflicting figure: a 2025 EDPB news item is titled "French SA fined KASPR €200 000", and CNIL has published a "closure of the order issued against KASPR". The amount may have been revised on appeal or in a later decision. Not verified. — [EDPB 2025 item](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/news/2025/data-scraping-french-sa-fined-kaspr-eu200-000_en); [CNIL closure notice](https://www.cnil.fr/en/closure-order-issued-against-kaspr)
- UK Upper Tribunal, *ICO v Clearview AI* (judgment 7 October 2025, [2025] UKUT 319 (AAC)) reversed the First-tier Tribunal and held the ICO had jurisdiction over US-based Clearview. "Behavioural monitoring" under UK GDPR Art 3(2)(b) was read broadly to include passive collection, sorting, classification and storage of data with a view to later profiling. Clearview was given permission to appeal to the Court of Appeal on 19 December 2025. — [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/10/uk-upper-tribunal-hands-down-judgment-on-clearview-ai-inc/); [Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/regulatory-investigations-financial-crime-insights/2025/10/the-reach-of-gdpr-and-the-readth-of-behavioural-monitoring.html); [Burges Salmon](https://www.burges-salmon.com/articles/102lr4m/clearview-ai-tribunal-takes-a-clear-view-on-icos-jurisdiction/); [ICLR judgment](https://www.iclr.co.uk/document/2025036946/2025ukut319aac_TNA/html)
- Global privacy regulators, including the ICO and Canada's OPC, issued joint statements on data scraping (August 2023; concluding statement 28 October 2024). Publicly accessible personal information is still subject to data-protection law in most jurisdictions. — [OPC concluding statement](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-news/speeches-and-statements/2024/js-dc_20241028/); [ICO news](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2024/10/global-privacy-authorities-issue-follow-up-joint-statement-on-data-scraping-after-industry-engagement/); [RPC](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/data-protection/autumn-2023/uk-ico-publishes-joint-statement-on-data-scraping-and-the-protection-of-privacy/)

**UK: database right and Computer Misuse Act**
- UK sui generis database right arises automatically where there is substantial investment in obtaining, verifying or presenting a database's contents. Extracting or re-utilising all or a substantial part without consent infringes it. The right lasts 15 years. — [Bird & Bird, "Legal weapons in the fight against data scraping"](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2021/global/legal-weapons-in-the-fight-against-data-scraping); [Sprintlaw UK](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/understanding-sui-generis-database-rights-in-the-uk/)
- The Computer Misuse Act 1990 criminalises unauthorised access. It has not been tested in a screen-scraping case, but it may catch scraping because the site owner does not authorise that kind of access. An "implied licence from public availability" argument is unlikely to succeed. — [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2021/global/legal-weapons-in-the-fight-against-data-scraping)

### Inferences
- **Settled (US):** logged-off collection of truly public pages is unlikely to be a CFAA crime in the Ninth Circuit. Logging in, creating fake accounts, or getting around technical blocks turns scraping into contract and possibly CFAA exposure (hiQ, Proxycurl).
- **Unsettled (US):** whether website terms bind a logged-off scraper through "browsewrap". *Bright Data* turned on Meta's specific terms and is one district-court ruling. Whether contract terms alone can "lower the gate" under the CFAA is still open after *Van Buren*.
- **Practical rule for this business:** do not scrape LinkedIn or Indeed, logged in or out, for a product you sell. The terms forbid it, LinkedIn litigates, and EU/UK regulators treat LinkedIn-derived contact data as a known enforcement target (Kaspr). Keep the repo's `linkedin_jobs` and Apify presets out of paid deliveries, as `CONTEXT.md` already advises.
- **UK/EU:** copying a substantial part of a job board's or data vendor's database (Adzuna is a UK company) can infringe database right even when no contract applies. Pulling individual employers' own ATS feeds is lower-risk than bulk extraction from an aggregator.
- **Clearview's reach:** a founder outside the UK/EU who compiles profiles of UK/EU people for resale should assume UK/EU GDPR applies, especially after the Upper Tribunal's broad reading of "monitoring". The Court of Appeal outcome is pending.

### Gaps
- Meta is widely reported to have dropped its remaining claims against Bright Data in February 2024. Not verified this session.
- No UK court decision applying the Computer Misuse Act to web scraping was found. The CMA position is untested.
- Whether any court has held that LinkedIn's *logged-off* public-profile data can be scraped and resold in the face of its current terms: no post-2022 merits ruling was found.
- Clearview's appeal to the Court of Appeal: outcome not found as of this research.

## 3. Privacy law for B2B contact data (UK/EU, US, Canada, Australia)

### Takeaway
Names, job titles, work emails and LinkedIn URLs are personal data in every target market. The "B2B" label reduces some marketing-consent rules but not the data-protection duties of a reseller.

**UK/EU:** legitimate interests can support compiling and selling B2B data. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 confirms direct marketing can be a legitimate interest. But a reseller must give Article 14 notices to the people it lists: case law and enforcement (Bisnode, Kaspr, Experian) show the "disproportionate effort" and "privacy notice on the website" routes are narrow when you hold people's emails. PECR lets clients email *corporate* subscribers without consent but not sole traders or ordinary partnerships, and PECR fines now reach £17.5m / 4% of turnover.

**US:** California's B2B exemption ended on 1 January 2023. A business that makes 50% or more of its revenue from selling personal information is a CCPA "business" whatever its size. Selling data about people you have no direct relationship with makes you a "data broker", which requires annual registration (California, $6,000-$6,600 for 2026) and, from 1 August 2026, processing deletion requests from California's DROP platform. Texas, Oregon and Vermont have their own registries.

**Canada and Australia:** B2B carve-outs exist (PIPEDA business-contact exemption, CASL and Spam Act conspicuous-publication rules), but they are lost or weakened when addresses are harvested or generated by software. Australia's small-business exemption does not cover businesses that trade in personal information.

### Cited Findings

**UK and EU GDPR: lawful basis**
- DUAA 2025 s.70 amends UK GDPR Art 6 to say explicitly that direct marketing is capable of being a legitimate interest under Art 6(1)(f). PECR consent and soft opt-in rules still apply to electronic mail. — [LegalVision UK](https://legalvision.co.uk/data-privacy-it/data-use-access-act-2025/); [Clarkslegal](https://clarkslegal.com/insights/articles/pecr-fines-direct-marketing-and-cookies/)
- DUAA received Royal Assent on 19 June 2025. Most provisions took effect on 5 February 2026, and the ICO confirmed on 19 June 2026 that all data-protection provisions had commenced. — [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/data-use-and-access-act-2025-duaa-compliance/); [Clifford Chance, Feb 2026](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2026/02/key-aspects-of-the-data--use-and-access--act-take-effect.html)
- For B2B data, legitimate interests is the standard UK GDPR lawful basis. B2C electronic marketing generally also needs PECR consent. — [Fieldfisher on Experian](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/insights/the-transparency-and-legitimate-interests-battle-in-direct-marketing-ico-to-appeal-first-tier-tribunal-decision-in-experian-v-the-information-commissioner-2023) (search extract)

**UK and EU GDPR: Article 14 transparency and data brokers**
- 2020: after investigating data brokers, the ICO issued an enforcement notice to Experian's marketing-services arm over transparency and over using legitimate interests for data originally collected with consent. — [Bird & Bird (2020)](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2020/uk/icos-investigation-into-the-data-broking-sector-and-enforcement-notice-to-experian)
- February 2023: the First-tier Tribunal overturned most of that notice. — [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102i8l8/a-sigh-of-relief-for-data-brokers-tribunal-overturns-most-of-icos-experian-en/); [Fieldfisher](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/insights/the-transparency-and-legitimate-interests-battle-in-direct-marketing-ico-to-appeal-first-tier-tribunal-decision-in-experian-v-the-information-commissioner-2023)
- 22 April 2024: the Upper Tribunal dismissed all five of the ICO's grounds of appeal (*ICO v Experian* [2024] UKUT 105 (AAC)). The appeal mainly concerned transparency under Art 5(1)(a) and Art 14. — [National Archives judgment](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ukut/aac/2024/105); [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/b/upper-tribunal-dismisses-ico-u-k-s-appeal-in-experian-case); [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/experian-appeal-guidance-gdpr-transparency); [Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/client-alert-navigating-gdpr-transparency-insights-from-the-upper-tribunal-in-the-appeal-against-experian); [ICO statement](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2024/04/ico-statement-on-upper-tribunal-ruling/)
- Poland, Bisnode (UODO, March 2019): a data analytics firm that compiled data from public registers was found in breach of Art 14. It had emailed notices to about 682k people for whom it held emails but not to about 6.5M people for whom it held only postal addresses, relying on "disproportionate effort" and a website notice. UODO rejected both. — [Blake Morgan](https://www.blakemorgan.co.uk/digital-marketing-agency-bisnode-fined-by-the-polish-dpa-for-failing-to-be-transparent-with-data-subjects/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/03/30/covert-data-scraping-on-watch-as-eu-dpa-lays-down-radical-gdpr-red-line/)
- Bisnode on appeal: the administrative court annulled the fine over how affected people were counted, but upheld that people with active businesses had to be notified under Art 14. The Supreme Administrative Court dismissed Bisnode's cassation appeal. — [UODO](https://uodo.gov.pl/en/553/1572); [Blake Morgan](https://www.blakemorgan.co.uk/digital-marketing-agency-bisnode-fined-by-the-polish-dpa-for-failing-to-be-transparent-with-data-subjects/)
- KASPR (CNIL 2024): not informing data subjects, English-only notices and 5-year retention were each part of the sanctioned breaches (see section 2). — [CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/en/data-scraping-kaspr-fined-eu240000)
- GDPR Art 14(3) sets the notice timing when data is not collected from the person: within a reasonable period and at the latest one month; at the first communication if the data is used to contact them; or at the latest when first disclosed, if disclosure to another recipient is planned. Art 14(5)(b) gives the "impossible or disproportionate effort" exception. — [GDPR Art 14 text](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-14-gdpr/) (statutory text; not fetched this session)

**UK PECR: B2B email (relevant to what clients do with the lists)**
- The PECR electronic-mail marketing rule does not apply to corporate subscribers (companies, LLPs, Scottish partnerships, public bodies), so B2B marketing emails can go to them without consent. Sole traders and some partnerships count as individual subscribers and need consent or the soft opt-in. — [ICO B2B marketing guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/); [ICO PECR e-mail rules](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guidance-on-direct-marketing-using-electronic-mail/how-do-we-comply-with-the-pecr-electronic-mail-marketing-rules/)
- Some commentators say emailing a named person at a corporate address may be contacting an individual subscriber. This is disputed, and the ICO's framing ties subscriber status to the corporate body. — [ConsentTrail](https://consenttrail.co.uk/blog/b2b-email-marketing-opt-out-rules) (secondary source; conflicts with the plain reading of [ICO B2B guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/business-to-business-marketing/))
- Under DUAA, maximum PECR fines rose from £500,000 to the higher of £17.5m or 4% of global annual turnover, matching UK GDPR. — [MFMac](https://www.mfmac.com/insights/data-protection/data-use-and-access-act-2025-increased-maximum-fines-for-cookies-and-direct-marketing-practices/); [Clarkslegal](https://clarkslegal.com/insights/articles/pecr-fines-direct-marketing-and-cookies/)

**US: CCPA/CPRA**
- The CCPA's temporary employee and B2B exemptions expired on 1 January 2023. B2B contacts who are California residents now have full rights to access, delete and opt out. — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2022/10/california-consumer-privacy-act-employee-and-b2b-exemptions-expire-january-1-2023); [California Lawyers Association](https://calawyers.org/privacy-law/hr-employee-data-b2b-data-to-come-within-scope-of-ccpa-on-january-1-2023/)
- A for-profit business is covered by the CCPA if it meets any one test: annual gross revenue above $26,625,000 (2025-26 inflation-adjusted), personal information of 100,000 or more California consumers or households, or **50% or more of annual revenue from selling or sharing personal information**. — [CPPA monetary thresholds](https://www.cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html); [SecurePrivacy](https://secureprivacy.ai/blog/ccpa-requirements-2026-complete-compliance-guide); [Clym](https://www.clym.io/blog/ccpa-applicability-guide)

**US: California Delete Act and data broker registration**
- A "data broker" is a business that "knowingly collects and sells to third parties the personal information of a consumer with whom the business does not have a direct relationship". Data brokers must register with the Agency (now branded CalPrivacy) by 31 January each year and pay an annual fee. — [CPPA data broker page](https://cppa.ca.gov/data_brokers/); [CPPA announcement Dec 2025](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2025/20251217.html)
- The 2026 fee is reported as $6,000 by one source and $6,600 by another (conflict; check the registry page). — [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/california-privacy-agency-launches-data-broker-strike-force-amid-delete-act-crackdown) ($6,000); [TrustArc](https://trustarc.com/resource/california-delete-act-drop-platform-data-brokers/) ($6,600)
- Brokers that miss registration face fines of $200 per day. The CPPA has created a Data Broker Enforcement Strike Force and says it will pursue these penalties. — [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/california-privacy-agency-launches-data-broker-strike-force-amid-delete-act-crackdown); [CPPA Enforcement Advisory 2025-01](https://cppa.ca.gov/pdf/enfadvisory202501.pdf)
- Enforcement advisories warn against "hiding the ball": unlisted trading names or websites, or relying on a parent's registration. A September 2026 advisory addresses the accuracy of registration information. — [CPPA Advisory 2025-01](https://cppa.ca.gov/pdf/enfadvisory202501.pdf); [CalPrivacy Advisory 2026-01](https://privacy.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/357/2026/09/Enforcement-Advisory-No.-2026-01-Accuracy-of-Data-Broker-Registration-Information.pdf)
- DROP (Delete Request and Opt-out Platform) launched on 1 January 2026. From **1 August 2026**, registered brokers must log in to DROP at least every 45 days, retrieve matching deletion requests, process them within 45 days and report status back. — [TrustArc](https://trustarc.com/resource/california-delete-act-drop-platform-data-brokers/); [Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/drop-is-coming-due-what-californias-delete-act-means-for-data-brokers-in-august/); [Alston & Bird Aug 2026](https://www.alston.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/california-privacy-opt-out-signals-data-brokers); [CalPrivacy data brokers](https://privacy.ca.gov/data-brokers)
- Commentators say B2B data companies should review whether they are data brokers, and that many companies qualify without realising it. — [California Lawyers Association, "Wake Now, Discover That You Are a Data Broker" (Jan 2025)](https://calawyers.org/privacy-law/wake-now-discover-that-you-are-a-data-broker/); [Coblentz Law](https://www.coblentzlaw.com/news/navigating-californias-data-broker-requirements-in-2026/)

**US: other state data broker registries**
- Texas and Oregon passed data broker registration laws, joining California and Vermont. — [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20231214-texas-and-oregon-adopt-new-rules-for-data-broker-laws); [Cozen O'Connor](https://www.cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2024/new-data-broker-laws-in-texas-and-oregon)
- Texas registers brokers with the Secretary of State for a $300 filing fee. 2025 amendments broadened the "data broker" definition while arguably narrowing the applicability thresholds. H.B. 1620 recodified the chapter (509 to 510) from 1 September 2025 without substantive change. — [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/texas-legislature-amends-data-broker-law-broaden-definition-arguably-narrow); [Privacy World](https://www.privacyworld.blog/2025/07/texas-legislature-amends-data-broker-law-to-broaden-definition-arguably-narrow-applicability-thresholds/); [Monda](https://www.monda.ai/blog/data-broker-registries-in-the-us)
- Oregon (HB 2052, ORS 646A.593, in force 1 January 2024) registers brokers with the Department of Consumer and Business Services, Division of Financial Regulation. The fee is $600, with penalties up to $500 per day capped at $10,000 per year. Its definition largely follows Vermont and California: a business that sells or licenses personal data of people it has no direct relationship with. — [Monda](https://www.monda.ai/blog/data-broker-registries-in-the-us); [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20231214-texas-and-oregon-adopt-new-rules-for-data-broker-laws)
- Comparative analysis of the four regimes. — [California Lawyers Association](https://calawyers.org/privacy-law/data-broker-regulation-framework-a-comparative-analysis-of-california-texas-vermont-and-oregon/)

**US: CAN-SPAM (applies to clients' outreach)**
- FTC compliance guide for CAN-SPAM. From background knowledge, not verified this session: the guide says the Act makes no exception for business-to-business email, and it requires accurate headers, non-deceptive subject lines, identification as an ad, a physical postal address, a working opt-out honoured within 10 business days, and responsibility for vendors who send on your behalf. Per-email civil penalties are inflation-adjusted each year (above $50,000 per email in recent years). — [FTC CAN-SPAM Compliance Guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)

**Canada: PIPEDA and CASL**
- PIPEDA exempts business contact information (name, title, business address, phone, email) collected, used or disclosed solely to communicate with the person about their employment or profession. — [Lexology, "What Exemption?"](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=1b300c34-ad21-422b-9d46-f68e70792a6b); [OPC CASL help](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/r_o_p/canadas-anti-spam-legislation/casl-compliance-help-for-businesses/)
- Where an address was "harvested" by software that scours the internet, the sender cannot rely on the PIPEDA exemption, even if the address is public and the message is about the person's job. — [McMillan LLP, "Contact Information Posted on Websites Not Necessarily Up for Grabs"](https://mcmillan.ca/insights/contact-information-posted-on-websites-not-necessarily-up-for-grabs/)
- CASL implies consent to commercial email when an address is conspicuously published, the publication carries no "no CEMs" statement, and the message relates to the person's business role. — [CRTC guidance on implied consent](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/com500/guide.htm); [Gowling WLG](https://gowlingwlg.com/en/insights-resources/guides/2023/doing-business-in-canada-casl)

**Australia: Spam Act and Privacy Act**
- The Spam Act allows inferred consent from conspicuous publication of employees', directors' or officers' work addresses, provided there is no "no spam" statement and the message relates to the person's published role. Consent cannot be inferred merely because an address is published somewhere. — [Pointon Partners](https://pointonpartners.com.au/spam-act/); [ALRC on the Spam Act](https://www.alrc.gov.au/publication/for-your-information-australian-privacy-law-and-practice-alrc-report-108/73-other-telecommunications-privacy-issues/spam-act/); [AI Point](https://www.aipoint.io/blog/cold-email-in-australia-what-the-spam-act-and-privacy-act-require)
- Privacy Act s 6D(4): a business falls outside the small-business exemption, whatever its turnover, if it trades in personal information, meaning it discloses personal information for a benefit without consent or legal authorisation. A change in force from 1 July 2026 brings more small businesses into the Act, and the government has agreed in principle to remove the exemption. — [Artificer Legal](https://artificer.legal/changes-to-small-business-privacy-obligations)

### Inferences
- **UK/EU, settled:** legitimate interests can support B2B list compilation and sale (Experian UT; DUAA s.70). A written legitimate interests assessment is still expected.
- **UK/EU, less settled:** *how* a reseller meets Art 14. Experian won on its facts: a large credit-reference agency with layered notices. Bisnode and Kaspr show regulators reject "website notice only" when the firm holds a direct contact channel. A business that holds each person's work email can send a short Art 14 notice cheaply, so a "disproportionate effort" argument is weak. Art 14(3)(c) points to notifying people **before or at the first sale to a client**.
- **California:** a solo reseller whose main revenue is selling contact files likely meets the CCPA's 50%-of-revenue test for any California residents in its files, regardless of size. It also fits the Delete Act "data broker" definition, since it has no direct relationship with the listed people. Expect roughly $6,000+ per year in registration, a DROP account, 45-day deletion cycles from August 2026, and $200/day exposure for not registering. This alone may make selling California-resident contacts uneconomic at the start. **Excluding California residents from delivered files** is a legitimate early-stage option.
- **Texas, Oregon, Vermont:** registration is cheaper ($300-$600) but each has its own definition and thresholds. They could not be confirmed in detail here, so check before selling contacts located in those states.
- **Canada and Australia:** the repo's default of **software-generated pattern emails** (`first.last@domain`) looks close to the "harvested or generated address" category that removes the PIPEDA business-contact exemption. It is also not "conspicuous publication" under CASL or the Spam Act, so clients may lack implied consent to email those addresses. Treat pattern-guessed addresses as unsuitable for Canadian and Australian recipients unless verified against a conspicuous public source.
- **CAN-SPAM and PECR bind the clients' outreach, not the file sale itself.** A reseller can still reduce joint risk by contractually requiring clients to comply, and by flagging sole traders or partnerships (UK) and Canadian or Australian contacts.

### Gaps
- Exact current Texas thresholds after the 2025 amendment, Vermont's fee and "brokered personal information" definition, and whether any state excludes pure business-contact data: not verified. Check the Texas Secretary of State, Oregon DCBS and Vermont Secretary of State sites.
- Delete Act independent audits (reported to start in 2028) and the exact per-request fine for not honouring deletions: not verified this session.
- The search extract attributed the doubling of the registration fine ($100 to $200/day) to SB 361 (2025). Other accounts tie the $200/day figure to the original Delete Act (SB 362, 2023). Unresolved.
- The FTC CAN-SPAM page could not be fetched, so the exact 2026 per-email penalty is unverified.
- The Experian First-tier Tribunal's residual order (the part of the ICO notice left standing, reportedly about notifying people not yet sent a privacy notice) was not confirmed in detail.
- ICO data-protection fee tiers and whether an overseas founder needs a UK/EU representative (Art 27): not researched this session. Founder location is not stated in the repo.
- Stricter EU national rules for B2B email, such as Germany's UWG s.7 prior-consent requirement: not researched. Treat EU markets beyond UK and France as unmapped.
- Australia: Spam Act Part 3 prohibitions on address-harvesting software and harvested-address lists, and whether pattern generation counts: not verified.

## 4. Practical compliance checklist and risk map for a one-person business

### Takeaway
The cheapest defensible model: sell **company-level hiring signals** (employer, role, location, date first seen, link to the source posting) drawn from sources whose terms allow commercial reuse, and add **named contacts only where you have a resale-compatible source and a privacy setup**. That setup means an Art 14 notice, suppression, a DPA-style client agreement, short retention, and data-broker registration where needed. Start with UK and non-California US; add California, Canada, Australia and the EU only once registration and notice processes exist.

### Cited Findings
- Apollo and Hunter forbid resale; Adzuna needs written consent or a licence for ongoing commercial use. — [Apollo Terms](https://www.apollo.io/terms); [Hunter Terms](https://www.hunter.io/terms-of-service); [Adzuna API Terms](https://developer.adzuna.com/docs/terms_of_service)
- Public ATS APIs expose only employers' published postings, with no candidate data. — [Greenhouse Job Board API](https://docs.greenhouse.io/job-board.html); [Lever postings-api](https://github.com/lever/postings-api); [Ashby Job Postings API](https://developers.ashbyhq.com/docs/public-job-posting-api)
- Regulators sanction contact-data vendors for missing notices, for non-local-language notices, and for keeping data 5 years after each update. — [CNIL on KASPR](https://www.cnil.fr/en/data-scraping-kaspr-fined-eu240000)
- A website-only notice was rejected for a data compiler that could contact people. — [UODO (Bisnode)](https://uodo.gov.pl/en/553/1572)
- California data brokers must register annually, pay the fee, and process DROP deletions every 45 days from 1 August 2026. — [CPPA data brokers](https://cppa.ca.gov/data_brokers/); [TrustArc](https://trustarc.com/resource/california-delete-act-drop-platform-data-brokers/)

**Practical checklist (tiny business, Stage 0)**
1. **Pick licensable sources.** Get Adzuna's written commercial or data licence, or switch the default to Greenhouse/Lever/Ashby employer watchlists and your own research. Do not resell Apollo or Hunter output. Keep LinkedIn and Indeed scraping (`linkedin_jobs`, Apify presets) out of paid work. If you buy a dataset, get resale or redistribution rights in the signed order form.
2. **Attribute.** Add "Jobs by Adzuna" plus a link wherever Adzuna jobs appear in HTML or Excel outputs, and keep a source URL and first-seen date for every signal.
3. **Minimise.** Default to company-level signals. For people, keep only name, title, work email, work LinkedIn URL and the source of each field. No personal emails or phone numbers. Do not guess emails for sole traders or for Canadian and Australian contacts.
4. **Write a Legitimate Interests Assessment** (purpose, necessity, balancing) and a public **privacy notice** covering the Art 13/14 content: who you are, sources, categories, recipients (recruitment agency clients), lawful basis, retention, rights, the right to object, and a contact.
5. **Send Art 14 notices** to UK/EU people before or at the first delivery that includes them: a short email to the work address with a link to the notice and a one-click opt-out. Log when each notice was sent. Offer notices in the local language for EU markets (Kaspr).
6. **Run a suppression and objection process.** Every objection or deletion request goes into `leadgen suppress` (global) and is passed on to clients who already received the person. Add the missing "delete a person" command so erasure removes stored data, not just future use.
7. **Set retention.** Set a short retention period (for example, drop unsold or stale contacts after a few months). Kaspr's 5-year retention was sanctioned. Build the automatic purge the repo lacks.
8. **Sign a client agreement / data-sharing terms.** The client is an independent controller. It must comply with UK GDPR/PECR, CAN-SPAM, CASL and the Spam Act; honour opt-outs and pass them back to you; not resell; delete on request; and use the data only for recruitment-services B2B outreach.
9. **Decide on California.** Either register as a data broker (fee, DROP, CCPA notice and rights) or filter out California-resident contacts until revenue justifies it. Do the same check for Texas, Oregon and Vermont.
10. **Keep records.** Record the source and licence of each dataset, the LIA, the notice log, the suppression and deletion log, client agreements, and registrations (a simple Art 30-style record).
11. **Check the UK ICO data-protection fee** (not verified this session). Check whether an overseas founder needs a UK/EU representative.
12. **Get a one-off legal review** of the client terms and privacy notice before scaling. These notes are not legal advice.

- Whether sending Art 14 notice emails is itself "direct marketing" under PECR: likely not, if purely informational, but not researched.
- Whether UK, US or Canadian regulators have acted against a *small* B2B list reseller specifically (rather than large brokers): no example found.
